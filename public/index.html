Five paths long-time Huntington Beach owners consider

After many years in the same home, the question “What’s next?” can feel big. There is no single right answer, and nothing here asks you to decide anything.

How to use this guide

Words like this open a short, plain-English explanation. Tap one any time. You can always come back.

1

Stay and age in place

May fit ifYou love your home and neighborhood, it suits you physically, and you want to keep your low property tax base.

Things to weighUpkeep and repairs on an older home, stairs and bathroom safety, yard work, and having help nearby. Simple changes like grab bars, better lighting, or a first-floor bedroom can make a big difference. The Huntington Beach Senior Center in Central Park offers care management and referrals at (714) 536-5600.

2

Sell and buy something smaller nearby

May fit ifYou would like single-level living, less maintenance, or a lock-and-leave home, while staying close to friends, doctors, and routines.

Things to weighCapital gains on the sale, HOA dues and rules, and moving logistics. Proposition 19 can let you bring your current property tax base with you if you are 55 or older.

3

Sell and rent

May fit ifYou want flexibility, to be near family, or to try a new area before committing.

Things to weighRent increases over time, giving up the Prop 19 benefit if you don’t buy within two years of selling, and capital gains on the sale.

4

Keep the home for your heirs

May fit ifLeaving the home to family matters to you and someone may want to live in it.

Things to weighHeirs generally receive a “step-up” in basis that can erase most capital gains, but under Prop 19 their property tax may rise sharply unless a child moves in. A living trust can avoid probate.

5

Stay and draw on your equity

May fit ifYou want to remain at home but would like more monthly cash flow.

Things to weighA federally insured reverse mortgage (HECM) is available at 62 and older. For 2026, FHA counts home value up to $1,249,125 when calculating how much you can access. Costs include an upfront insurance premium (2% of that value), you must keep paying property taxes, insurance, and upkeep, and HUD-approved counseling is required first.

Proposition 19: your tax base can move with you

Many long-time owners stay put because they fear a much higher property tax bill on their next home. For owners 55 and older, Prop 19 often removes that worry.

The short version

If you are 55 or older, you can generally carry your current taxable value to a replacement primary residence anywhere in California, up to three times, if you buy within two years before or after selling.

Who qualifies

  • Homeowners 55 or older, or severely disabled, or whose home was lost to a wildfire or disaster
  • The home you sell must be your primary residence, and so must the new one
  • The new home can be anywhere in California, in any county
  • You buy or build within two years before or after the sale

If the new home costs the same or less

Your full low tax base comes with you. If you buy after selling, “the same or less” stretches a little: up to 105% of your sale price in the first year, and 110% in the second.

An example

Your home’s taxable value is $300,000. You sell it for $1,400,000 and buy a single-level condo for $1,100,000.

Yearly tax without Prop 19, about
$12,650
Yearly tax with Prop 19, about
$3,450
Savings each year, about
$9,200

Illustration only, using a rate of about 1.15%. HOA dues and special assessments are separate.

If the new home costs more

You still benefit. The difference in price is added to your old tax base. In the example, a $1,600,000 home would be taxed on $300,000 + $200,000 = $500,000, not on $1,600,000.

Don’t miss the paperwork

The benefit is not automatic. You file a claim with the Orange County Assessor (form BOE-19-B), generally within three years of buying. Official details are at boe.ca.gov/prop19.

Capital gains: know your number before you sell

After decades in Huntington Beach, your home has likely grown in value far more than you paid. Knowing your number ahead of time takes the surprise out of selling.

The short version

You can exclude up to $250,000 of gain from tax, or $500,000 for qualifying married couples filing jointly. Long-time owners often have gains far above that. Records of improvements you’ve made can lower your taxable gain dollar for dollar.

Things to know

  • To use the exclusion, you generally must have owned the home and lived in it for at least two of the last five years.
  • California taxes the gain too, as regular income. Together, federal and state tax can take a meaningful share of the taxable gain.
  • Surviving spouses can usually still use the full $500,000 exclusion if they sell within two years of a spouse’s passing. The step-up may also apply to the home.
  • A second home or rental follows different rules. See what changes.

Part 5 of 5

Passing your home to family, and your next steps

The short version

Heirs usually inherit at today’s value, which can eliminate decades of capital gains. Gifting the home during life generally loses that benefit. Under Prop 19, heirs keep your low property tax only if a child moves in, and only up to a limit.

Three things to understand

  • The step-up in basis can save your heirs a large capital gains bill.
  • Prop 19 for heirs can raise their property tax sharply if no child moves in.
  • A living trust can keep your home out of probate court.

A gentle suggestion

Before deciding anything, gather three things. With them in hand, a CPA, an estate attorney, and a local Realtor can each give you real numbers instead of guesses.

Helpful local and official resources

  • Huntington Beach Senior Center in Central Park: 18041 Goldenwest St., (714) 536-5600. Classes, transportation, and care management.
  • California State Board of Equalization: boe.ca.gov/prop19 for official Proposition 19 information.
  • Orange County Assessor: questions about your taxable value and Prop 19 filings.
  • HUD-approved housing counselors: required before any reverse mortgage; find one at hud.gov.

Capital gains

What counts as an improvement

The short version

Money you spent making your home better, bigger, or longer-lasting is added to what you paid for it. That lowers your taxable gain dollar for dollar. Everyday repairs don’t count.

Usually counts

  • Room additions, a remodeled kitchen or bathroom
  • A new roof, windows, or heating and air conditioning
  • A pool, patio, block wall, or landscaping project
  • Solar panels, new plumbing or electrical, a garage conversion

Usually does not count

  • Painting, fixing a leak, or replacing a broken part
  • Routine upkeep and cleaning

Missing old receipts?

That is common after many years. City permit records, old photos, bank statements, and contractor names can help your CPA rebuild the list. Start gathering now, well before you sell.

If you own more than one property

When you own a second home or rental

The short version

Many of the benefits in this guide apply only to the home you live in. A rental or second home follows different rules for capital gains, property tax, and your heirs.

What changes

  • No $250,000 / $500,000 exclusion. The full gain may be taxable when you sell.
  • Depreciation is taxed back. If you claimed depreciation on a rental, part of the gain is taxed at a different rate.
  • Prop 19 doesn’t carry over. Neither the move-your-tax-base rule nor the parent-child rule applies, so the property is reassessed when it passes to heirs.
  • The step-up still applies when heirs inherit.

Options worth asking about

  • A 1031 exchange can defer the tax by trading into another investment property.
  • Holding it until heirs inherit can erase the gain through the step-up.
  • Making sure it is in your living trust keeps it out of probate.

Stay and draw on your equity

How a reverse mortgage (HECM) works

The short version

A reverse mortgage lets homeowners 62 and older turn part of their home’s value into cash, with no monthly mortgage payment. The loan is repaid when the last borrower moves out or passes away, usually from the sale of the home.

Things to know

  • You keep the title and stay in your home as long as it is your primary residence.
  • You must keep paying property taxes, homeowners insurance, and upkeep.
  • Costs: an upfront insurance premium of 2% of the home’s value (counted up to $1,249,125 in 2026), plus an ongoing yearly premium, interest, and closing costs.
  • Your heirs are protected. They never owe more than the home is worth, and can keep the home by paying off the loan.
  • Counseling first. A session with a HUD-approved counselor is required before you apply.
  • “HECM for Purchase” can help you buy a new, easier home without a monthly payment.

Keeping the home for your heirs

The “step-up” in basis

The short version

When your children inherit your home, the tax law generally treats them as if they bought it at its value on the day they inherit. Decades of growth in value can be wiped clean for capital gains purposes.

An example

You bought your home in 1985 for $180,000. Today it is worth $1,400,000.

If you sell it yourself, your gain is roughly
$1,220,000
Less the $250,000 exclusion (single)
−$250,000
Gain that may be taxed
$970,000
If your heirs inherit and sell soon after at $1,400,000
About $0

Illustration only, before improvements and selling costs. Your CPA can run your real numbers.

Things to know

  • Giving the home away while you are living usually loses the step-up. Your children would take on your original purchase price instead.
  • Married couples in California may get a step-up on the whole home when the first spouse passes, if it is held as community property.
  • The step-up is about income tax, not property tax. Property tax for heirs is a separate rule under Prop 19.

Questions to bring to your CPA

  1. What is my current basis, including improvements I have made?
  2. How is title held, and does that affect the step-up?
  3. Is there anything I am planning that would lose it?

Keeping the home for your heirs

Prop 19 and your heirs’ property tax

The short version

Before 2021, children could usually keep their parents’ low property tax. Now they keep it only if a child moves into the home as their main residence, and only up to a limit. Otherwise the home is reassessed at today’s value.

What has to happen to keep the low tax

  • It must be your family home, your primary residence.
  • A child (or in some cases a grandchild) must move in within one year and file for the homeowners’ exemption.
  • The low value carries over only up to your taxable value plus $1,044,586 (through February 15, 2027). Value above that is added to the tax bill.
  • Rentals and second homes are reassessed when they pass to heirs.

What it can mean in dollars

A home with a taxable value of $250,000 pays about $2,900 a year in property tax. If heirs who don’t move in inherit it at a market value of $1,400,000, the bill can rise to about $16,000 a year.

Illustration only, using a rate of about 1.15%. Actual rates and assessments vary.

Official information: boe.ca.gov/prop19 and the Orange County Assessor.

Keeping the home for your heirs

Living trusts and avoiding probate

The short version

A living trust holds your home while you are alive, and you stay in full control. When you pass, the person you chose hands it to your family directly, without going through probate court.

Why probate matters in California

  • It takes time. Probate often runs a year or more, and the home can be tied up while it does.
  • It is public. Court filings can be seen by anyone.
  • Fees are set by state law and based on the home’s full value, with no reduction for a mortgage.

California statutory probate fees on a $1,000,000 estate

Attorney
$23,000

$23,000

Things to know

  • A trust only helps if the home is actually titled in the trust’s name. Many people sign a trust and never move the deed into it.
  • If you own more than one property, each one needs to be in the trust.
  • Putting your home in your own living trust generally does not change your property tax or the step-up.

What to have ready for an estate attorney

  1. Your most recent property tax bill for each property
  2. Your grant deed, or how title is currently held
  3. Any existing will or trust, even an old one
